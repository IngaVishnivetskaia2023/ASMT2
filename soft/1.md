### 1. Task Delegation
Delegation is transferring **ownership** and **decision-making authority** for a task, not just handing off work. 

#### Can be delegated: 
- technical decisions within defined scope, 
- code reviews,
- spike investigations,
- documentation,
- mentoring juniors,
- running standups.

#### Cannot be delegated: 
- final accountability (you're still responsible for the outcome),
- performance reviews,
- confidential matters,
- tasks requiring your specific authority level.

The Delegation Poker / 7 Levels of Delegation range from **"Tell"** (I decide, you execute) through **"Consult"** (I decide after hearing you) to **"Delegate"** (you decide and act, just inform me). When delegating, always clarify the expected outcome, constraints, deadline, and reporting frequency — not the exact steps (that would be micromanagement). The appropriate delegation level depends on the delegate's competence and commitment (Situational Leadership model — D1 through D4).
Always provide context (the WHY) so the person can make **autonomous decisions** when unexpected situations arise.

#### 2. Delegation vs Assignment
Assignment is giving a person a defined task with clear steps — "implement this API endpoint per this spec." 
The assignee **executes but doesn't own decisions**. 
Delegation transfers **authority and ownership** — "you own the authentication module, decide the approach, report back on progress." 
❗ In assignment, the HOW is defined by you; 
in delegation, the WHAT is defined by you but HOW is decided by the delegate. 
Assignment is appropriate for junior developers who need structure (Situational Leadership S1 — Directing). 
Delegation is appropriate for mid/senior developers who have competence and need autonomy (S3-S4 — Supporting/Delegating). 
The key distinction: assignment develops execution skills, delegation develops decision-making and ownership skills. 
Moving team members **from receiving assignments to receiving delegations** is a core part of professional growth and empowerment.


#### 3. Task Decomposition & Managing Growth Ambitions

Decompose complex tasks using vertical slicing — **each slice delivers end-to-end value** — rather than horizontal splitting (frontend/backend separately). 

When a less senior teammate wants complex tasks, apply **scaffolded delegation**: 
- assign a well-defined subtask of the complex feature,
- provide guardrails (architecture decisions pre-made, interfaces defined),
- set check-in points (not just deadline),
- and review incrementally.

Create **a spike task first** — "spend 4 hours researching technology X, present findings" — this lets them learn without risking delivery. 
Use pair programming on the complex parts: they drive, you navigate — transferring knowledge while controlling risk. 
If the full task is too risky, split it: they do the new-technology PoC, you integrate it into production. 
Always frame it positively: "I want you to succeed on this, so let's build up to it with these steps" — this is growth-oriented feedback rather than gatekeeping. 
Track their progress through competency matrix to make growth visible and objective.


#### 4. Motivation Techniques
Use **intrinsic** motivation over **extrinsic** — Daniel Pink's framework: 
- Autonomy (let them choose HOW to solve),
- Mastery (progressively challenging tasks, visible skill growth),
- Purpose (connect their work to user/business impact).

Recognize contributions publicly and specifically — "your refactoring reduced production bugs by 40%" is better than "good job." 
Provide **stretch goals** — tasks slightly above current skill level that promote flow state (Csikszentmihalyi) — not too easy (boredom) and not too hard (anxiety).
For mentees specifically, make progress visible: "three months ago you couldn't do X, now you own it" — this builds self-efficacy. 
Use 1-on-1 meetings regularly to understand individual motivators — some want technical depth, others want leadership exposure, others want work-life balance. 
Remove demotivators first (unclear requirements, constant context-switching, blame culture) — Herzberg's hygiene factors must be met before motivators work. 
Create psychological safety (Amy Edmondson) where mistakes are learning opportunities, not punishment triggers.

#### 5. Handling Team Conflicts
First, identify the conflict type: 
- task conflict (disagreement on technical approach) is healthy;
- relationship conflict (personal friction) is destructive.

For technical disagreements, facilitate a structured discussion: each side presents arguments with data/evidence, team evaluates against objective criteria (performance benchmarks, maintainability, alignment with architecture). 
Use ADR (Architecture Decision Record) to document the decision and reasoning — this prevents revisiting resolved conflicts. 
If conflict is interpersonal, address it early and privately — unresolved conflicts compound (Thomas-Kilmann model: avoiding is the worst strategy). 
Apply active listening: restate each person's position to ensure they feel heard before seeking resolution. 
As a mediator, stay neutral — focus on shared goals ("we both want clean code and fast delivery") rather than positions. 
If conflict persists, escalate to manager/DM — not as failure but as responsible impediment removal.

#### 6. Dealing with Junior Refusing Boring Tasks
First, **empathize and acknowledge** — "I understand this isn't exciting, and I value your ambition." 
Then provide **context and purpose** — explain WHY this task matters: "fixing these 20 lint warnings improves our CI pipeline reliability and teaches you the codebase faster than any complex feature would." 
**Reframe** "boring" as foundational: "senior developers who skipped fundamentals have gaps that hurt them later — this builds your professional foundation." Apply **task rotation** — balance boring tasks across the team fairly so no one person gets all the tedious work; 
use a visible rotation system for transparency. 
**Offer a trade:** "complete this task by Wednesday, and Thursday-Friday you can work on the spike for the new feature." 
If resistance continues, have a direct but respectful 1-on-1: explain that professional maturity includes doing unglamorous work, and that reliability on simple tasks is what earns trust for complex ones. 
Document these conversations — if the pattern persists, it becomes a performance and attitude concern to address formally.

#### 7. Dealing with Low Employee Performance
Start with **root cause analysis** — is it a skill gap, motivation issue, personal problem, unclear expectations, or wrong role fit? 
Have a private 1-on-1 — use SBI feedback model: 
- Situation (specific context),
- Behavior (what you observed),
- Impact (how it affects team/project).

Set a **Performance Improvement Plan (PIP)** with SMART goals: 
- Specific,
- Measurable,
- Achievable,
- Relevant,
- Time-bound — "complete 3 stories per sprint with zero rework for the next 4 weeks."

Provide support resources: mentoring, pair programming, training time, reduced WIP to help them focus. 

Check in frequently (weekly or bi-weekly) — don't wait until PIP deadline to give feedback. 
Document everything — conversations, agreements, progress — this protects both parties. 
If no improvement after the agreed period, escalate to management with documented evidence — this is not failure, it's responsible leadership. 
Remember: keeping a low performer without addressing it demotivates the entire team.


#### 8. Onboarding Process & Documentation
Onboarding goals: 
- reduce time-to-productivity,
- prevent knowledge silos,
- build team belonging,
- and establish quality standards from day one.

Structure onboarding in phases: 
- Day 1 (environment setup, access, meet the team),
- Week 1 (architecture overview, first small task, assign buddy/mentor),
- Month 1 (independent delivery on medium tasks, understand full SDLC flow).

Onboarding documentation should include: 
- project description and business context,
- architecture diagrams (C4 model),
- module ownership map,
- ADRs for non-obvious decisions,
- local setup guide (step-by-step, tested regularly),
- CI/CD pipeline description,
- branching strategy (GitFlow/trunk-based),
- environment map (dev/QA/staging/PPD/prod),
- contribution guidelines (PR process, commit conventions, DoD).

Use a checklist tracked in Jira/Confluence — onboarding is a process, not a one-time meeting. 
Assign a buddy (peer-level, not manager) for safe questions. 
The best test of onboarding docs: can a new person run the project locally without asking anyone? 
Review and update docs every onboarding cycle — the newcomer should improve docs for the next person.

#### 9. Developing and Mentoring Team Members
Mentoring is a long-term relationship focused on professional growth, not just solving today's problem. 
Use Individual Development Plans (IDP): 
- identify current level,
- target level,
- skill gaps,
- and concrete actions to close gaps within a timeline.

- Apply **70-20-10 model**: **70%** learning from **challenging tasks** (**stretch assignments**), **20%** from **social learning** (code reviews, pair programming, knowledge sharing), **10%** from **formal training** (courses, certifications).

During code reviews, don't just fix — explain the WHY: "this approach violates SRP because..." — turning every review into a micro-learning moment. Create safe failure space: let them attempt hard tasks in non-critical paths, review together, iterate — experiential learning is the strongest retention method. Use Socratic method: instead of giving answers, ask guiding questions: "what happens if this endpoint gets 1000 concurrent requests?" — building critical thinking, not dependency on you. Track growth against competency matrix and celebrate milestones publicly — visible progress fuels motivation.

#### 11. D2 vs D3 Developer
D2 (Mid-level) executes independently on well-defined tasks — they know **HOW** to build features, write tests, and follow established patterns within the existing architecture. 

D3 (Senior) **operates with ambiguity (uncertainty)** — they **can take an unclear business need, decompose it, design the approach, and deliver end-to-end with minimal guidance**. 
D2 asks "how should I implement this?"; 
D3 asks "should we implement this at all, and if so, what's the optimal approach?" 

D3 demonstrates technical leadership: 
- proposing architectural decisions,
- mentoring others,
- identifying and addressing tech debt proactively,
- participating in requirements clarification.

D3 has cross-functional impact: 
- they improve not just their own code but team processes,
- documentation,
- code quality standards,
- and onboarding.

D3 takes ownership beyond their ticket: 
- monitoring production quality,
- suggesting process improvements,
- facilitating knowledge sharing.

The key shift from D2 to D3 is from **individual contributor excellence** to **team-level multiplier** — a D3 makes the entire team better, not just their own output.

#### 12. Interviewing Candidates & Defining Level
Use a structured interview with consistent evaluation criteria across all candidates — avoid gut feeling. 
Split assessment into dimensions: 
- technical skills (coding, architecture, problem-solving),
- communication (clarity, active listening),
- collaboration (teamwork, conflict resolution),
- ownership (initiative, accountability).

For technical assessment, use open-ended problems that reveal thinking process, not trivia: "design a caching strategy for this scenario" shows more than "what is closure?" 

Define level using competency matrix: 
- Junior executes defined tasks with guidance;
- Mid executes independently with some ambiguity;
- Senior handles full ambiguity, mentors, and influences architecture.

Use behavioral questions (STAR method: Situation, Task, Action, Result): "tell me about a time you disagreed with a technical decision" reveals seniority better than algorithm puzzles. 
Always leave time for candidate's questions — what they ask reveals their maturity and priorities. Calibrate with other interviewers using scorecard (1-5 per dimension) before discussion to avoid anchoring bias.

#### 13. Time Management Techniques
Use Eisenhower Matrix to **prioritize**: 
- Urgent+Important (do now),
- Important+Not Urgent (schedule),
- Urgent+Not Important (delegate),
- Neither (eliminate).

Apply **time-boxing**: 
- allocate fixed time to activities — "1 hour for code review, then stop" — prevents Parkinson's Law (work expands to fill available time).

**Protect deep work blocks:** 2-3 hour uninterrupted coding sessions with notifications off — context switching costs 20-30 minutes to recover (Cal Newport's Deep Work). 
**Use daily planning:** 10 minutes each morning to identify top 3 priorities — if nothing else gets done, these three matter most. 

**Combat meeting overload:** decline meetings without agenda, propose async communication (Slack/Confluence) for status updates, batch meetings into one part of the day. 
For estimates, apply buffer principle: estimate then add 20-30% — accounts for interruptions, reviews, unexpected issues. 
Track where time actually goes for one week — most people discover 30-40% of time goes to **unplanned interruptions**, which enables corrective action.


#### 14. Effective Meetings
Every meeting **must have an agenda** shared in advance — no agenda, no meeting. 

**Define timeframe upfront** (30/45/60 min) and respect it — assign a timekeeper if needed. 

Identify **required vs optional attendees** — don't invite people "just in case"; respect their time. 
Use facilitation techniques: keep discussion on topic, park tangents in a parking lot (list for later), give quiet people space to speak. 

End every meeting with MFU (Minutes, Follow-ups, Action items): what was decided, who does what, by when — shared in writing within 24 hours. 

For decision meetings: present options with pros/cons beforehand, meeting is for discussion and decision only — not for first-time presentation. 

For recurring meetings (standups, retros): evaluate regularly — "is this meeting still valuable?" — cancel or reduce frequency if not. 

The two-pizza rule (Bezos): if two pizzas can't feed the group, too many people are in the meeting.

#### 15. Negotiation Techniques
Start with **interest-based negotiation** (Harvard method): focus on **underlying interests** ("I need reliable delivery") **not positions** ("I want 3 more developers"). 

Always prepare: know your **BATNA (Best Alternative to Negotiated Agreement)** — your walkaway option gives you power. 

Use **active listening first**: understand the other side's constraints and motivators before presenting your case — people are more receptive after feeling heard. 

Frame **proposals as win-win**: "if we reduce scope by 20%, we deliver 2 weeks earlier with higher quality — client gets faster time-to-market" rather than "we can't do this." 
Use **data and evidence over opinions**: "our throughput data shows 12 SP/sprint, so 40 SP needs 3.5 sprints" is harder to argue against than "I think we need more time." 

Apply **anchoring**: present your preferred option first — it sets the reference point for negotiation. 

Know when to escalate vs compromise — **not every negotiation needs a win**; sometimes strategic concession on a small point builds trust for bigger asks later.
